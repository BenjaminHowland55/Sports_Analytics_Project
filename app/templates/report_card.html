How the Grades Work

Grading a player

  1. Opportunities. For each game: pass snaps = defensive snaps × the team's share of plays that were dropbacks, run snaps = defensive snaps − pass snaps, and coverage snaps = pass snaps − blitzes. Only games that have been charted count, so each rate's top and bottom cover the same games.
  2. Stats. Each stat is events ÷ opportunities (table below). A stat is used for a room only if it measures part of that room's job and repeats for the same player from one season to the next with a correlation (r) of at least 0.15. Pressure rate is left out for linebackers and defensive backs because it mostly shows how often they are sent on blitzes. Tackles count through stops: tackles that leave the offense with a failed play (it lost expected points) without a loss of yards, with an assist counting as half. Only 43% of tackles are stops; the rest come after the offense has already gained enough, so counting every tackle would reward tackles made 15 yards downfield. Stops are compared with what an average player in the room would make in the same run and pass snaps, because a linebacker makes about three times as many stops per run snap as per pass snap; otherwise a linebacker who drops into coverage a lot would look worse.
  3. Three-season blend, pulled toward average. The window is this season plus the player's two most recent healthy seasons before it (8+ games; a season lost to injury is skipped and an earlier one used, looking back up to four seasons). Each season is weighted by how long ago it was: 3 for this season, 2 for last season, 1 for two seasons ago, ½ for three, ¼ for four, so skipping an injured season doesn't promote an older one to a recent season's weight. recent rate = Σ weight × events ÷ Σ weight × opportunities, then blended rate = (recent rate × evidence + k × league rate) ÷ (evidence + k), where evidence = Σ (weight ÷ 3) × opportunities and k = average opportunities × (1 − r) ÷ r. The noisier a stat, the bigger k, so a small sample is pulled harder toward the league rate. This season's opportunities count in full and older ones less, so a rookie isn't treated as having more sample than he has, and a player whose best play is years old (out injured since) is pulled further toward average. Players who missed most of one or two seasons since 2021 then played on return about where this predicted. In testing, the blend predicted the next season better than the last season alone for every stat.
  4. z-score. z = (blended rate − room average) ÷ room standard deviation, using the room's busier half to set the average and spread. The sign is flipped for lower-is-better stats, so a positive z is always good. z is capped at ±2, so one stat can't dominate: without the cap, six forced fumbles over three seasons scored +3.5 and put a linebacker at #1 on that alone. The cap made every room's grades match team results the same or better.
  5. Weights: what the NFL pays for. For every veteran contract signed since 2019 (rookie deals left out), the player's z-scores from the three seasons before signing are compared with the contract's yearly value as a share of the salary cap. A regression finds how much extra pay each stat brings while holding age fixed. Weights are kept from going negative, since overlapping stats can otherwise make it look like teams pay for missing tackles. weight = extra % of the cap paid for one standard deviation better at the stat. 200 resamples of the contracts give the 90% range shown for each weight.
  6. Snap share. How much of the defense's snaps a player is trusted to play reflects what coaches see on every snap of film, and it's a big part of pay. It counts only the games he dressed for (played on defense or special teams), so games missed hurt don't count against him, but being benched does. Adding it made the rankings line up far better with who gets paid most (rank match with veteran pay: edge 0.56 → 0.84, corners 0.23 → 0.66, linebackers 0.17 → 0.68) and made the edge and corner grades match team results better. For linebackers and safeties it crowded out the stats that separate them on the field, so its weight is capped at 20% there, and at 30% for the interior line.
  7. Corners are the exception. Interceptions swing a lot from season to season, so instead of following pay alone, lockdown coverage (yards per coverage snap and completion % allowed) gets 50% of the corner grade left after snap share. Within the lockdown stats, and within the rest, each stat keeps the market's proportions, so interceptions still count the most among the impact plays.
  8. Composite. composite = Σ (weight × z) ÷ Σ weight.
  9. Grade (0–100). The composite's percentile among players in the same room with 100+ snaps this season. Players on a roster with 800+ snaps over the three seasons who are under 100 snaps this season (hurt, or just back) are still graded on that track record and listed, tagged "Injured reserve" or "Limited this season".
  10. Trend. Each season in the player's window is graded on its own, as a percentile among that season's players in the room with a full sample (8+ games; this season, 100+ snaps and at least half the games so far). Every player is pulled toward average by the same amount here, so missing games doesn't by itself make a player look like he declined. Players 26 and older: a rise each season = Improving, a drop each season = Declining, anything else = Steady. Players 25 and under get room on the way down, since grades typically move about 20 points from one season to the next: any rise in grade counts as improvement, and so does a season his snap share rose 25+ points (the coaches trusting him with more) unless his grade fell more than 20; a smaller drop counts as no change. A young player with any improvement and no drop of more than 20 is Improving. With two seasons, the one change decides it; with one, no trend.

Grading a room and its contracts

  1. Room score = the snap-weighted average composite of everyone who has played in the room this season. Players with no track record count as average.
  2. Cap spent = the sum of the players' contract value per year ÷ the salary cap in the year each contract was signed, so older and newer deals compare fairly.
  3. Value = room score − the room score expected for that spending, from a straight-line fit across the 32 teams. Positive = more grade than the money usually buys.
  4. Rooms. Players are placed by their Over The Cap contract position, except that linebackers who mostly rush the passer count as edge rushers, and edge rushers who mostly drop into coverage count as linebackers (based on how often they are targeted and how often they create pressure).
  5. Confidence. Each room was graded using only earlier seasons, with that season's roster, for 2020–2025 and compared with the team's actual results that season. The confidence label reflects how well that matched.
