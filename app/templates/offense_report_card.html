Offensive Lines (Graded as Units)

Free data has no individual blocking stats, so each team's line is graded as a whole on {{ card.season }} results. Pass blocking = pressure rate allowed (sacks + QB hits per dropback, 50%) and sack rate allowed (50%), each adjusted for the quarterback's average time to throw: a quarterback who holds the ball longer takes more pressure and sacks whatever the line does, so each rate is compared with what's expected at that time to throw (this matched the team's passing results better). Run blocking = adjusted line yards (40%: a running back's yards credited to the line in steps, with losses counting 120%, the first 4 yards 100%, yards 5–10 half, and nothing beyond 10), yards before contact per running back carry (30%), and stuff rate (30%: runs stopped at or behind the line). Overall = the average of the two. These stats repeat moderately from season to season (0.28–0.41) and match the team's passing and running EPA well (0.47–0.74). Running backs affect the run numbers too: a strong back gains more before contact.

How the Grades Work

The offense is graded the same way as the defense; the defensive Report Card has each formula in full. In short:

  1. Stats. Each stat is events ÷ opportunities (table below), used only if it measures part of the room's job and repeats for the same player from one season to the next with a correlation of at least 0.15, with the exceptions noted. Shares (carries, targets, air yards) are the offensive version of tackles: the volume a team trusts a player with, counted in the games he played.
  2. Blend. This season plus the player's two most recent healthy seasons (8+ games), weighted 3, 2, 1 by how many seasons ago (½ and ¼ further back), and pulled toward the league rate by k = average opportunities × (1 − r) ÷ r, with older seasons counting as less evidence.
  3. z-score against the room's busier half, sign flipped for lower-is-better stats. Only the noisiest stats (repeat r below 0.20: interception rate, rushing EPA, fumbles and touchdown rates) are capped at ±2. Capping everything tied the best players on stats they're reliably far ahead on (a tight end with 29% of his team's targets got the same credit as one with 21%). Most offensive per-play stats repeat around 0.2–0.4, so the defense's 0.40 cut-off would cap nearly all of them; 0.20 kept every check the same as no cap.
  4. Weights: what the NFL pays for. Every veteran contract signed since 2019 (rookie deals left out) is compared with the player's z-scores from the three seasons before signing; a regression holding age fixed, with weights kept from going negative, gives each stat's share. 200 resamples give the 90% range.
  5. Quarterbacks. Pay alone put about 60% of the grade on touchdown, interception and sack rates, which swing from season to season and lean on the line and play-calling. So efficiency (EPA per play and completion % over expected) is guaranteed half the grade, with the rest keeping the market's proportions. EPA per play combines passing and rushing over all of a quarterback's plays, so running counts by its real volume rather than equal to passing. Snap share is left out (starters play nearly every snap, so it mostly measured being rested in blowouts), and this season counts 2.5 instead of 3, since seven games is a small sample at the most important position. Average depth of target and deep-ball accuracy are included; they made every check slightly better. Adjusting EPA for pressure faced and receivers' yards after the catch was tested and left out (it predicted the next season worse), and so were clutch stats: EPA late in close games barely repeats from season to season, EPA on third and fourth down added nothing pay didn't already see, and win probability added mostly reflects which moments a quarterback happened to play in. Tracking and charting stats were also tried across the offense (rush yards over expected, separation, yards after the catch over expected, contested catches, catchable-ball rate, interception-worthy throws, time to throw, and others) and left out.
  6. Offensive line. Graded as units, not players: the free data has no individual blocking stats (who allowed a pressure or missed a block), and grading linemen from their team's results while they were on the field ranked good lines rather than good linemen. See "Offensive Lines" above for the formulas.
  7. Composite, grade, injured players and trend work exactly as on the defensive Report Card.
